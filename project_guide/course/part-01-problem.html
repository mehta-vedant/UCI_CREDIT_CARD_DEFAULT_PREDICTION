1. What problem are we solving?

Begin with the business question before talking about code or algorithms.

IMPLEMENTED IN THIS PROJECT

Default prediction, not fraud detection

A credit card lets a bank pay a merchant now while the cardholder repays the bank later. Each month, the client receives a bill. A default means the client does not meet the repayment obligation. This repository predicts the column default.payment.next.month: whether a historical client defaulted in the following month.

Fraud is different. If someone steals a card and buys a phone, the question is “was this transaction authorised?” Fraud systems often need a decision in milliseconds and use transaction details such as merchant, time, location, device, and spending pattern. Our dataset has client credit limit, repayment status, bills, and payment amounts. It has no individual transactions. Calling it a fraud project would be inaccurate.

The plain-English question

Given a client's credit limit, demographic codes, six months of repayment status, six bill amounts, and six payment amounts, estimate the chance that they will default next month.

Think of a tutor looking at a student's previous homework and attendance before estimating whether the student will pass the next test. The tutor sees patterns, not the future. The estimate can be wrong. The model plays the same role with historical examples.

Why use machine learning?

A bank could write one rule: “if a customer missed a payment, flag them.” That rule ignores degree and context. Someone who was two months late repeatedly, has high recent bills relative to their limit, and pays little may be different from someone with one unusual late month and otherwise regular payments. Machine learning learns a numerical relationship from many labelled examples instead of relying on one hand-written rule.

Who benefits, and what can go wrong?

A careful risk estimate can help a lender decide which accounts deserve a human review, support, or a different credit strategy. It cannot replace a fair lending policy or a person. A false negative misses someone who later defaults. A false positive inconveniences someone who would have paid. Those two errors have different costs, which is why the application reports a probability and uses a deliberate review threshold rather than treating every score as certain.

This is an educational risk-estimation system. It must not be used by itself to automatically deny credit. The academic dataset is historical, limited, and does not establish whether a decision would be fair or lawful in a real setting.

Check yourself

Why is this not a fraud detector?

It predicts next-month payment default from client history. It does not contain transaction-level evidence of an unauthorised purchase.

What does a probability of 0.30 mean?

For a row with similar inputs, the model estimates 30% default risk. It does not mean default is guaranteed, and it does not describe a person's worth or intent.

Why not use one IF statement?

Many signals interact. The trained model can use repeated lateness, bill amounts, payments, and derived history together.
