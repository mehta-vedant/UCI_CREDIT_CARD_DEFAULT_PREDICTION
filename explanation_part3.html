1 What is Feature Engineering?

Feature engineering is the process of using domain knowledge to create new input variables (features) from raw data that make machine learning algorithms work better. It is widely considered the most important step in the ML pipeline — because even the most sophisticated model can only be as good as the data it sees.

Chef Analogy

Think of a chef working with raw ingredients. A carrot, a potato, and an onion are ingredients (raw features), but slicing them into the right shapes, marinating them, and combining them in the right way is what turns them into a gourmet dish. Feature engineering is exactly that — it transforms raw data columns into meaningful signals that a model can learn from. A model trained on raw bill amounts alone will struggle; a model trained on "credit utilization" (bill ÷ credit limit) immediately understands spending behavior.

Why does feature engineering matter so much?

  • Raw data is rarely in the right format. The UCI dataset gives us raw bill amounts and payment amounts, but the relationship between them (the ratio) is what actually predicts default.
  • Models need context. A bill of ¥50,000 means nothing without knowing the credit limit. An average delay of 2 months tells a story that 6 individual month columns cannot easily convey.
  • Domain knowledge creates shortcuts. Instead of forcing the model to learn that “high bills with low payments = danger,” we hand-craft that signal directly.
In this project: We start with 24 original features and engineer 17 new features, bringing the total to 41+ features that give the model a much richer understanding of credit risk.

3 Every Engineered Feature Explained

Below we walk through every single feature created by the CreditCardFeatureEngineer class. Each one is presented with its formula, intuition, and a concrete example.

#1 credit_utilization
credit_utilization = BILL_AMT1 / LIMIT_BAL   (if LIMIT_BAL > 0, else 0)
Wallet Analogy

Imagine you have ¥10,000 in your wallet (credit limit). If you've already spent ¥8,000 (bill amount), you've used 80% of your wallet. That's a high utilization — you're living on the edge. Someone who's only spent ¥1,000 (10% utilization) has much more breathing room.

Why it predicts default: People who max out their credit limits are under heavy financial pressure and are far more likely to miss payments. This single feature is often the strongest predictor of default.

Example Calculation

BILL_AMT1 = ¥45,000   |   LIMIT_BAL = ¥80,000

credit_utilization = 45,000 / 80,000 = 0.5625 (56.25%)

Div-by-zero protection: If LIMIT_BAL is 0 (a degenerate case), the code returns 0 instead of dividing by zero:

X_copy['credit_utilization'] = np.where(
    X_copy['LIMIT_BAL'] > 0,
    X_copy['BILL_AMT1'] / X_copy['LIMIT_BAL'],
    0
)
#2-7 payment_ratio_1 through payment_ratio_6
payment_ratio_i = PAY_AMT_i / BILL_AMT_i   (if BILL_AMT_i > 0, else 0)
Bill-Paying Analogy

You receive a restaurant bill of ¥1,000. If you pay ¥900, your payment ratio is 0.9 — you covered 90% of the bill. If you only pay ¥100, the ratio is 0.1 — you're barely keeping up. If your ratio consistently drops below 0.5 month after month, you're headed toward default.

Why it predicts default: These ratios reveal payment behavior over time. A person who consistently pays less than what they owe is accumulating debt. The pattern of these ratios across 6 months tells a much richer story than any single month's payment.

#8 avg_payment_status
avg_payment_status = mean(PAY_0, PAY_2, PAY_3, PAY_4, PAY_5, PAY_6)
Credit Report Card Analogy

Think of your payment history as a 6-month report card. Each month you get a score: -1 (paid early, gold star), 0 (on time, good), 1 (one month late, warning), 2 (two months late, failing). The average gives you an overall grade. An average of 0.5 means you're generally a bit late. An average of -0.3 means you're usually ahead of schedule.

Why it predicts default: This collapses 6 months of payment status into a single number that captures the overall payment discipline. A positive average means chronic lateness — a strong default signal.

Example Calculation

PAY_0=-1, PAY_2=0, PAY_3=1, PAY_4=2, PAY_5=1, PAY_6=0

avg_payment_status = (-1+0+1+2+1+0) / 6 = 0.50

#9 max_payment_delay
max_payment_delay = max(PAY_0, PAY_2, PAY_3, PAY_4, PAY_5, PAY_6)
Flood Water Analogy

Imagine a river monitored for 6 months. The average water level matters, but what really determines whether the dam breaks is the highest water level. Similarly, one extremely late payment (e.g., 8 months overdue) is a massive red flag, even if other months look fine.

Why it predicts default: The worst delinquency often indicates the point of no return. A max delay of 8 months (code 8 in the dataset) almost certainly means the customer is in serious trouble.

Example Calculation

PAY values: [-1, 0, 1, 2, 1, 0]

max_payment_delay = 2 (worst case: 2 months late)

#10 consecutive_delays
consecutive_delays = count(PAY_i > 0 for each month)
Tardiness Tracker Analogy

Imagine tracking how many times someone was late to work out of 6 months. Being late once might be a fluke. Being late 5 out of 6 months is a pattern. This feature counts the number of months where the customer was delinquent (PAY > 0 means "payment was late by N months").

Why it predicts default: Frequency of delinquency matters as much as severity. A person who was 1 month late six times is in a very different situation from someone who was 6 months late once. This captures the chronic nature of payment problems.

Why it predicts default: High average bills suggest high spending relative to income, which increases default risk. This feature gives the model a single "spending level" signal rather than requiring it to compare 6 individual columns.

Mountain Climbing Analogy

Imagine plotting your monthly bills on a graph — month 1 on the left, month 6 on the right. Now draw the best straight line through those points. If the line goes up (positive slope), your bills are increasing — you're spending more each month. If the line goes down (negative slope), your bills are shrinking — you're paying down debt. A steep positive slope is a danger signal.

Why it predicts default: A rising bill trend suggests the customer is accumulating more debt over time. A declining trend suggests they're getting their finances under control. The direction and magnitude of this trend is highly predictive.

4 Credit Utilization Deep Dive

Credit utilization deserves special attention because it is consistently one of the top predictors of credit card default across many studies and datasets.

The Wallet Test

Imagine two people each have a ¥100,000 credit limit. Person A has ¥10,000 on their current bill. Person B has ¥90,000. Who is more likely to default? Person B, because they've already used 90% of their available credit. They're one emergency away from being unable to pay. Credit utilization is the ratio of how much you've used to how much you have.

The Two Forms

Continuous: credit_utilization

BILL_AMT1 / LIMIT_BAL

Captures the exact level of utilization. A value of 0.95 is worse than 0.85. The model can learn the gradient of risk across all utilization levels.

Binary: high_utilization

1 if utilization > 0.8 else 0

Captures the hard threshold. In credit scoring, the jump in risk above 80% is often nonlinear — a binary flag helps the model capture this discontinuity.

Why 0.8 (80%)?

The 80% threshold is well-established in credit risk literature:

  • Most financial advisors recommend keeping utilization below 30% for a good credit score
  • Risk increases significantly above 50%
  • At 80%+, the probability of default spikes dramatically
  • The 0.8 threshold was likely validated through the EDA process in Part 2, where default rates were observed to jump sharply at this level
Both Features Work Together

The continuous feature lets the model learn nuanced patterns (e.g., risk doubles from 0.5 to 0.9), while the binary feature provides a clean signal for the tree-based models to make sharp splits at the critical threshold.

src/components/data_transformation.py : 87-148 
class DataTransformation:

    def get_data_transformer_object(self):
        numerical_columns = [
            'LIMIT_BAL', 'AGE',
            'BILL_AMT1', ..., 'BILL_AMT6',
            'PAY_AMT1', ..., 'PAY_AMT6',
            'PAY_0', 'PAY_2', ..., 'PAY_6'
        ]

        categorical_columns = ['SEX', 'EDUCATION', 'MARRIAGE']

        engineered_numerical_columns = [
            'credit_utilization', 'avg_payment_status', 'max_payment_delay',
            'avg_bill_amount', 'bill_amount_trend', 'avg_payment_amount',
            'payment_amount_trend', 'months_with_delays'
        ] + [f'payment_ratio_{i}' for i in range(1, 7)]

        engineered_categorical_columns = ['high_utilization', 'ever_paid_early']

        # Numerical pipeline: fill missing with median, then standardize
        num_pipeline = Pipeline(steps=[
            ("imputer", SimpleImputer(strategy="median")),
            ("scaler", StandardScaler())
        ])

        # Categorical pipeline: fill missing with mode, one-hot encode, then scale
        cat_pipeline = Pipeline(steps=[
            ("imputer", SimpleImputer(strategy="most_frequent")),
            ("one_hot_encoder", OneHotEncoder(drop='first', handle_unknown='ignore')),
            ("scaler", StandardScaler(with_mean=False))
        ])

        # Combine into a ColumnTransformer
        preprocessor = Pipeline(steps=[
            ("feature_engineer", CreditCardFeatureEngineer()),
            ("column_transformer", ColumnTransformer(
                transformers=[
                    ("num_pipeline", num_pipeline, all_numerical_columns),
                    ("cat_pipeline", cat_pipeline, all_categorical_columns)
                ],
                remainder='drop'
            ))
        ])
        return preprocessor

Why These Specific Choices?

SimpleImputer(strategy="median") for Numericals

Why median, not mean? The median is robust to outliers. If one customer has a BILL_AMT of ¥5,000,000 (an outlier), the mean would be pulled upward, distorting the imputation. The median ignores this outlier and fills missing values with the true center of the distribution. In credit data, outliers are common — making median the safer choice.

StandardScaler for Numericals

Why scale? Tree-based models (like XGBoost) technically don't require scaling since they split on feature values regardless of scale. However, scaling helps when:

  • Regularization is applied (penalties are sensitive to feature magnitudes)
  • Comparing feature importances across features with different scales
  • The model pipeline is shared with non-tree models (like logistic regression)

StandardScaler transforms each feature to have mean=0, standard deviation=1, putting all features on a comparable scale.

OneHotEncoder(drop='first')

Why drop='first'? This is the dummy variable trap avoidance. If EDUCATION has values [1, 2, 3, 4], one-hot encoding would create 4 binary columns. But these 4 columns are perfectly correlated (if you know 3 are 0, the 4th must be 1). Dropping the first category breaks this perfect multicollinearity, reducing redundancy.

handle_unknown='ignore': If the test set contains a category not seen during training, it's silently encoded as all zeros rather than raising an error.

StandardScaler(with_mean=False) for Categoricals

After one-hot encoding, the categorical columns are sparse binary vectors (mostly 0s and 1s). with_mean=False means it only scales by standard deviation (divides by std), without centering (substracting mean). Centering sparse binary data would destroy its sparsity and create an unnecessarily dense matrix.

ColumnTransformer: Different Processing for Different Types

Numerical columns need median imputation and standard scaling. Categorical columns need mode imputation, one-hot encoding, and variance scaling. ColumnTransformer applies the right pipeline to the right columns simultaneously, keeping the code clean and the transformations type-appropriate.

Fit on Train, Transform on Test (Data Leakage Prevention)
src/components/data_transformation.py : 200-201 
# fit_transform on training data (learns statistics + applies transformation)
input_feature_train_arr = preprocessing_obj.fit_transform(input_feature_train_df)

# transform ONLY on test data (uses statistics learned from training)
input_feature_test_arr = preprocessing_obj.transform(input_feature_test_df)

This is critical. If we fit on the test set, the scaler would learn the test set's mean and standard deviation — information the model shouldn't have access to during training. This is called data leakage and it artificially inflates model performance. By only calling fit_transform on training data and transform on test data, we ensure the test set is a true proxy for unseen future data.

Exam Analogy

It's like a student studying for an exam. The training set is their textbook — they learn the mean, median, and patterns from it. The test set is the actual exam — they apply what they learned, but the exam questions are new. If the student had already seen the exam answers (fit on test), their score would be meaningless.

remainder='drop'

The ColumnTransformer has remainder='drop', which means any columns not explicitly assigned to a pipeline are dropped. This is how the ID column and target column are excluded — they're not in either the numerical or categorical column lists, so they're automatically removed.

10 Why These Specific Features?

Each engineered feature isn't arbitrary — it's grounded in domain knowledge about credit risk and informed by the EDA findings from Part 2. Here's the justification for each engineering choice:

FeatureDomain JustificationEDA Connection
credit_utilization Credit utilization ratio is the #1 factor in FICO scores. It measures how close a borrower is to maxing out their credit. EDA showed that higher LIMIT_BAL groups have lower default rates — utilization normalizes bill amounts relative to credit limit.
payment_ratio_* Payment-to-bill ratio measures repayment capacity. Consistently low ratios indicate the borrower cannot service their debt. EDA revealed that PAY_AMT distributions are heavily skewed, with defaulters having significantly lower payment amounts relative to bills.
avg_payment_status Averaging 6 months of payment status captures overall payment discipline in a single number. EDA showed that PAY_0, PAY_2, PAY_3 etc. all individually correlated with default — aggregating them provides a robust combined signal.
max_payment_delay The worst delinquency is a strong signal of financial distress. Even one 6+ month delinquency is highly predictive. EDA likely showed extreme PAY values (>3) are rare but almost always associated with default.
consecutive_delays Frequency of delinquency indicates chronic vs. one-time issues. Chronic delinquency is a stronger predictor. EDA showed PAY value counts were non-uniform — more delinquent months = higher default rate.
avg_bill_amount Average monthly spending level provides context for the model. High spenders have different risk profiles. EDA showed BILL_AMT distributions differ between defaulters and non-defaulters.
bill_amount_trend Rising bills without rising income is unsustainable. Trend captures the trajectory toward potential default. EDA showed that defaulters tend to have increasing bill amounts in the later months.
payment_amount_trend Decreasing payments signal worsening financial health. The divergence from bill trends is especially powerful. EDA revealed that payment amounts have high variance — the trend smooths this into a directional signal.
high_utilization The 80% threshold is an industry-recognized danger zone for credit risk. EDA likely showed a nonlinear jump in default rates above 80% utilization, justifying a binary flag.
ever_paid_early Early payment is a strong positive signal of financial health and cash management. EDA showed that PAY values < 0 are associated with lower default rates — this feature captures that protective factor.
EDUCATION cleanup Unknown categories (0, 5, 6) add noise without signal. Grouping them into "Others" (4) reduces spurious patterns. EDA showed that categories 0, 5, 6 had very small sample sizes, making their individual default rates unreliable.
MARRIAGE cleanup Value 0 is undocumented. Merging with "Others" (3) avoids the model learning from a data entry error. EDA showed category 0 had negligible count — not enough to be a meaningful predictor on its own.
The Big Picture

Raw features tell you what happened (bills, payments, statuses). Engineered features tell you what it means (utilization, trends, patterns). The model doesn't need to discover the relationship between bill amount and credit limit — we hand it the answer as credit_utilization. We don't ask it to compare 6 months of payment history — we distill it into avg_payment_status and max_payment_delay. Good feature engineering reduces what the model needs to learn, making it more accurate with less data.

Key Takeaway

The 17 engineered features in this project transform 24 raw columns into a rich 41+ feature set that captures utilization patterns, payment behavior trends, delinquency severity and frequency, and spending trajectories. This is what separates a mediocre model from a high-performing one — not a fancier algorithm, but better input data.
